"This Ordinance applies to all immovable property the subject of the Land Law of 7th Ramadan, 1274 A.H." — Transfer of Land Ordinance, 1920: a British statute defining its own scope by a Hijri-dated Ottoman code. The Mandate never replaced Ottoman land law.

Every statute, translation, register, commission report, transaction and fatwa in the land regime is a dated artifact — so their citation and derivation relations form a directed acyclic graph. Drawn that way, the system's structure becomes computable: the 1858 Ottoman Land Code is the hub and the single point of failure; the deepest derivation chain runs through transactions and reactions, not legal doctrine; and the British administered the whole edifice through a two-step translation chain, Turkish → French → English. Edges are documented derivations only — verbatim citation down to historian attribution, each marked.

Seven findings

01
The Mandate never replaced Ottoman land law — structurally, not impressionistically
Every Mandate land instrument with a doctrinal parent has the 1858 Code as that parent. The Transfer of Land Ordinance defines its scope by "the Land Law of 7th Ramadan, 1274"; the Mewat Land Ordinance opens by citing Article 103 of the Code; Settlement of Title classifies parcels by the Code's own categories. British innovation is procedural (cadastre, settlement of title) — the substance stays Ottoman. The Code is simultaneously the graph's hub and its single point of failure.
02
The deepest chain is action–reaction across tracks, not doctrine-elaboration within one
The longest path runs legal enablement → market behavior → religious-political counter-mobilization → imperial policy reversal → restriction law: the 1856 reform pledge, the 1867 foreigners' law, two purchase waves, the 1935 fatwa, the Revolt, the 1939 White Paper, the 1940 Land Transfers Regulations, the 1946 Survey. Every purely legal chain is shorter. Responses to transactions, not legal development, propel the system.
03
A two-step translation bottleneck, attested at each hop
The Mandate administered Ottoman law through English texts derived from French texts derived from the Turkish: the Code → Young's Corps de droit ottoman (1905–06) → Fisher's Ottoman Land Laws (1919) — "based on the French translations in Mr. George Young's Corps," by Fisher's own preface. Two translators stand between the operative statute and every British land officer who applied it for thirty years.
04
The commission cascade mutates its central concept at every hop
1929 violence → Shaw → Hope Simpson (cites Shaw) → Passfield (walked back by the MacDonald Letter) → the French Reports → Peel → Woodhead → White Paper → LTR 1940. Along the chain, "landless Arab" narrows from Hope Simpson's structural 29.4% estimate to French's operationalized register — 3,271 claims, 899 accepted, ~74 families resettled — to Peel's reuse, to the White Paper's premise. Same label, four referents: a paraphrase chain running through official documents.
05
The doctrinal and empirical tracks first merge in 1928
Before the Mandate, the empirical track's only node is the tabu register record — which the Survey of Palestine itself calls "the merest guess." The Land (Settlement of Title) Ordinance 1928 is the first node with parents in both tracks (the Code's categories + Dowson's tenure studies + the cadastral survey) — which is exactly why parcel-level tenure becomes knowable only from that decade, and then for only ~18% of Palestine by 1945.
06
The Arab response track derives from transactions, never from statutes
Every node in the response track is parented by a sale — the Fula transfer → the al-ʿIsa petition to Istanbul; the sales waves → the 1925 and 1935 fatwas — and none by a British instrument. The registers engaged are Ottoman-constitutional (a mukhtar petitioning the capital) and Islamic-legal (the 1925 Gaza fatwa ruling that sellers' Jewish buyers had "ceased to be ahl al-dhimma"). Two decades into British administration, the land conflict's Arab-side intellectual history still ran in Ottoman and Islamic registers. (A structural observation on documented edges — not a claim about Arab engagement with Mandate institutions generally.)
07
The only non-state roots are the Zionist institutions — and they head the longest chain
Every other root is a state decree, a doctrine, or a shock. The JNF (1901) and Ruppin's Palestine Office (1907–08) enter with no in-window parents, connect to the state system almost solely through the 1867 enablement edge, and stand at the head of the system's longest derivation chain. Privately-rooted institutional initiative, legally enabled in 1867, is what the rest of the graph spends 1910–1940 responding to.
Discipline. Edges record documented derivation only — each carries a relation type and an evidence grade, from verbatim citation inside a statute text down to historian attribution; no "influence" edges without a documentary basis. Behavioral feedback (fatwa enforcement suppressing sales; restrictions reshaping purchase strategy) is real but lives outside the formalism: it surfaces as later dated nodes, never as back-edges — which is what keeps the graph acyclic by construction. Excluded for want of corpus attestation: Tute's 1927 commentary, a JNF-inalienability doctrine edge, and the muhajir grant regime (demographically minor west of the Jordan).

The sharpest single asymmetry. In the same decade, from the same origin empire: Muslim refugees from Russia (Circassians, post-1864) received free state land, cash allowance, oxen-and-seed grants and multi-year exemptions — while Jewish refugees from Russia (post-1881 pogroms) were barred from entering Palestine at all, and a decade later barred from buying miri land even as Ottoman subjects. The incorporation regime was religion-gated at both ends.
What the restrictions cost in practice — the surcharge economy. They did not stop Jewish acquisition; they re-priced and re-routed it: entry overland via Egypt; baksheesh at every node ("Everything had its price: entry and release of baggage at the ports, permits to buy land and build on it"); purchase registered in the names of Ottoman Jews, local Arabs "for a consideration," even consuls; "temporary structures" and dug-outs where permits were refused. The 420,600 dunams of 1914 — 1.6% of Palestine — were assembled through this regime: market-only, surcharged, nominee-mediated. Roughly what a single Beirut Christian family acquired by clean title in its first Jezreel transactions. One downstream effect: absentee-owned lowland tracts (one seller, one bribe-set, consular cover) were far more tractable than village-adjacent hill parcels — the restriction regime is an input to the lowland settlement geography the Peel line later traced. Constraint before preference.
Motivation vs incidence — the calibration. The Porte's stated reasons (told to Isaac Fernandez, 1882) were anti-nationalist and anti-capitulatory — fear of "another Rumanian or Bulgarian issue," and of multiplying Great-Power-protected subjects — and the same anti-autonomy reflex hit the Templers, whom the Porte long refused property titles despite the 1867 law, "fearing that they would make themselves independent of the national authorities." But the incidence was confessional: the 1881 and 1892 orders name Jews as such, sweep in Ottoman Jews with no national project, and have no Christian counterpart — the Templers faced no entry ban, no purchase ban, no nominee economy, and got their titles. Both facts stand: a general Ottoman reflex against concentrated foreign settlement, and a confession-specific restriction regime layered on one group. Ottoman weakness made Jewish acquisition possible despite that regime — not because of any welcome.

What the 47.79% is made of — the evidentiary ladder

The Arab headline figure ≈ 12.57 million dunums (~12,600 km²) — in a country only ~18% cadastrally settled. It is not registered title. The attribution rule, from the instrument's own compiler: settled title where Land Settlement had run; elsewhere "reputed ownership, that is, those persons who were in actual occupation and liable for the payment of the tax." The Survey's own revenue chapter calls the community split "estimated." The figure decomposes into three evidentiary rungs:

Is the communal rung "owned land"? Neither individually owned nor unencumbered state land — it is a dual-layer right. The bulk is metruke-class village commons: under the Land Code, villagers whose animals "have pastured ab antiquo… cannot be prevented from pasturing" — an enforceable communal usufruct on state substrate, inalienable, with no individual shares. A worked margin (terraced pockets classified non-cultivable by the fiscal test) could ripen into individual title by prescription; the mewat beyond was state outright, its cultivate-to-title route criminalized by the Mewat Land Ordinance 1921. The matrix itself betrays the artifact: the same commons appear in the Arab column where the cadastre had not arrived and in the State column (registered to the High Commissioner "in trust") where it had — the column split measures administrative progress, not rights. Usage rule: for individual, alienable ownership, the honest Arab figure is the ~22% rung; for legally recognized communal interests — the relevant frame for dispossession accounting — the rung counts fully. Both compressions ("Arabs owned 47.79%" / "it was just state land") fail the same test in opposite directions.

The honest back-cast. Nearly all Jewish purchase came from private and absentee owners, not the state route (the concessions, ~175,000 dunums, were the exception) — so the ex-Negev "functionally Arab-held" share at earlier dates is approximately the 1945 Arab share plus land later sold: roughly 87–88% of ex-Negev Palestine in Arab customary or registered hands in 1882, declining to 77.4% by 1945, with State/commons/forest holding ~11–12% throughout. That is arithmetic on the anchor plus a sourcing assumption — it cannot be verified against period registers, and it deliberately attempts no mulk/miri or registered/customary split.

What the record can and cannot say. From 1882 the corpus supports decade-resolution quantification of ownership by community (the Survey's Table 4 series, the JNF series, Village Statistics 1945). It supports quantification of tenure by legal category — how much land was freehold mulk versus state-held miri — in no decade whatsoever: no recording instrument existed before 1928, coverage reached ~18% by 1945 and was never published as an aggregate. The qualitative bound is three-source convergent (Survey; Granott; Stein): freehold was a small, mostly urban category; Arab farmland was overwhelmingly miri. Any source asserting a national mulk percentage for any decade is manufacturing precision the recording instruments of that decade cannot support.

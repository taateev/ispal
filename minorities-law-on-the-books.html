"Apartheid" is a legal-structure claim — an argument about what is written into law. So the honest test set is the law as written, on all sides of the region at once. This page lays out, clause by clause, what nineteen legal systems put on the books about religion and the citizen: state-religion and sharia clauses, religion-gated nationality, apostasy and blasphemy statutes, worship and marriage restrictions, the legal standing of each surviving Jewish community — and Israeli law held to identical scrutiny, including the West Bank's dual legal regime that the apartheid charge principally rests on. Everything quoted is instrument text; everything that is convention, practice, or unverified is marked as such.

II · Nationality & immigration — laws on outsiders, not on citizens scope: immigration

Everything in this section governs acquisition — who may enter and become a member. None of it touches the rights of anyone already a citizen: the Law of Return confers nothing on Israel's existing citizens, Jewish or Arab, and Jordan's art 3(2) sorted who was incorporated in 1954, not how Jordanian citizens are treated. Immigration is also the domain where states well beyond this region openly sort by ethnicity or religion — co-ethnic return and preference laws operate in Germany, Greece, Hungary, Armenia, Ireland. So the mirror below is like-for-like within this scope only: two acquisition laws keyed to Jewishness in opposite directions. Reading either as evidence about the treatment of citizens — in either direction — is a category error. The citizen-scope comparison is §§III–V; the non-citizen-resident scope, where the apartheid charge actually lives, is §§VI–VII.

Jordan · Nationality Law No. 6 of 1954, art 3(2) · in force
"The following shall be deemed to be Jordanian nationals: … Any person who, not being Jewish, possessed Palestinian nationality before 15 May 1948 and was a regular resident in the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan between 20 December 1949 and 16 February 1954."
The clause that naturalized the Palestinian refugee cohort — minus Jews. A one-time incorporation clause, not a continuing bar (the ordinary naturalization articles have no religion condition), but never repealed through the 2023 consolidations.
Israel · Law of Return 1950 §1 + Citizenship Law 1952 §2 · in force
"Every Jew has the right to come to this country as an oleh." … "Every 'oleh under the Law of Return shall become an Israel national."
Automatic and unconditional for Jews, their children, grandchildren and spouses (1970 §4A); everyone else passes through six cumulative naturalization conditions plus ministerial discretion — §5(b): the minister grants citizenship "if he thinks fit to do so." An acquisition law throughout: it neither adds to nor subtracts from the rights of any existing citizen.
Kuwait · Nationality Law 1959, art 4(5) · added 1981 · in force
"…that he be an original Muslim by birth, or that he has converted to Islam… Nationality thus acquired is ipso facto lost… if the naturalized person expressly renounces Islam…"
Naturalization is Muslim-only, and a convert's citizenship — and his dependants' — is void on apostasy. 300+ non-Muslim citizens naturalized before 1981 remain. Survived the 2024 amendments.
Yemen · Nationality Law 6/1990, art 5 · in force
"The Yemeni Nationality may be granted… to any Moslem Arab or foreigner…"
The third explicit statutory religion gate. Saudi Arabia, by contrast, has no religion clause in its nationality statute — the gate is an executive-regulation file requirement (imam's certificate) plus practice the state itself acknowledged in 1938: "actually a non-Moslem has never been naturalized." practice

A distinction the page preserves throughout: the entry bans target Israeli citizenship and travel documents, not Jewish identity as such — Jewish citizens of third countries are not barred by these instruments. IV · Marriage & the family — the shared Ottoman inheritance scope: citizens

One millet system, many heirs

No polity on this page except Tunisia performs civil marriage for all its citizens. Israel, Lebanon, Jordan, Syria, Egypt, the PA — all run marriage through religious-community courts, because all inherited the same Ottoman millet architecture (in Israel's case via the Palestine Order in Council 1922). Israel and Lebanon even share the workaround: neither performs civil marriage, both register foreign civil marriages (Israel: HCJ 143/62 Funk-Schlesinger; Lebanon: Decree 60 L.R./1936 — hence the Cyprus circuit from both Beirut and Tel Aviv). This column of the comparison is a shared regional legacy, not a distinguishing feature of either side.

Where the asymmetry is real: in every surveyed state but Tunisia, a Muslim woman's marriage to a non-Muslim man is void on the books (Jordan PSL art 33(1); Morocco art 39; Syria art 48(2); Iran CC 1059; Iraq PSL; Egypt via Hanafi default) — Tunisia abolished its bar (a 1973 ministerial circular, not a statute) in September 2017, the region's only abolition. Iran adds an inheritance rule with no Israeli analogue: Civil Code art 881 bis — a single heir who converts to Islam takes the entire estate of a Jewish or Christian decedent, excluding all other heirs. Israel's religious-court system carries its own bars — halakhic refusal of intermarriage, the agunah problem — but conversion, non-marriage cohabitation, and foreign civil marriage are all legal and state-registered, and a 2010 civil-union track exists for the religiously unaffiliated.

VI · Non-citizens — where the region's hardest exclusions live scope: residents

The harshest on-the-books exclusions in the region run on the citizen / non-citizen line rather than the confessional one — the same line the West Bank regime runs on. This scope, not §II's immigration preferences, is the load-bearing comparison set for the apartheid question, in both directions: Israel's entries here are the family-unification bar and the West Bank dual regime (§VII), not the Law of Return.

Lebanon · Law 296/2001, art 1 · Palestinians & property
"No real property right of any kind may be acquired by any person who does not hold a nationality issued by a recognized State, or where such acquisition contradicts the provisions of the Constitution with respect to the rejection of permanent settlement [tawteen]."
Names no nationality; describes exactly one population. Heirs of pre-2001 Palestinian owners cannot register inheritance. Alongside it: exclusion from ~36 syndicated professions (law, medicine, engineering) via nationality/reciprocity conditions a stateless person can never meet — the 2010 labor amendments did not touch the syndicates, and a 2021 ministerial opening was suspended by the Shura Council in 2022.
Israel · Citizenship & Entry Law 2003 / 2022 · in force
"…the Interior Minister shall not grant an inhabitant of an area [West Bank/Gaza — excluding the inhabitant of an Israeli settlement]… citizenship… and shall not give him a license to reside in Israel…"
The family-unification bar on Palestinian spouses (+ Iran, Lebanon, Syria, Iraq nationals), age-gated permit exceptions only; the 2022 re-enactment's purpose clause cites Israel as "a Jewish and democratic state." Lapsed 2021, re-enacted 2022, renewed since; petitions pending at the HCJ.

VII · Israel under the same lens — the two regimes citizens residents

Israel's instruments sort into the two scopes that matter: citizen-scope — the Nation-State clause, land-allocation structures, religious personal-status courts, and the equality statutes — and resident-scope — the regime over non-citizen Palestinians in the West Bank, which is where the apartheid characterization is actually pitched. Inside the Green Line, Israeli statute books carry both the Jewish-preference instruments quoted above and a body of anti-discrimination law with no regional counterpart (Women's Equal Rights 1951; Employment Equal Opportunities 1988 — race, religion, nationality among forbidden grounds; Products & Services 2000). The Admissions Committees Law 2011 carries both in one statute: §6C(C) forbids rejection "for reasons of race, religion, gender, nationality…" while §6C(A)(4)–(5) permit rejection for "social suitability" and "social-cultural fabric" — the open texture critics argue does the filtering the explicit clause forbids (upheld, HCJ 2311/11). The apartheid charge, though, principally rests across the Green Line — on a dual legal regime that is not in dispute as fact, only as characterization:

The structure · not in dispute
"A military court is authorized to adjudicate any offense defined in security legislation and law." — Order 1651 §10(A), the regime for West Bank Palestinians
"…the Knesset applied Israeli laws to settlers on a personal and extraterritorial basis… as well as to Jews to whom the Law of Return applies… even if they are not Israeli citizens. The national identity of a suspect or defendant determines which law will apply." — ACRI, One Rule, Two Legal Systems (2014)
Two bodies of law, two court systems, one territory, sorted by identity — the black-letter core both sides argue from.
The apartheid characterization

B'Tselem (2021): "one regime governing the entire area… based on a single organizing principle" of Jewish supremacy. HRW A Threshold Crossed (2021): the elements of "the crimes against humanity of apartheid and persecution" are met. Amnesty (2022): "a system of oppression and domination… wherever it has control over their rights."

The rebuttal

Occupation law itself (Hague Reg. 43; GC IV art 64) requires the occupant to keep the local law in force rather than extend its own — separate systems are what the law of occupation mandates. Oslo II art XVII(2)(c), signed by the PLO: Palestinian Council jurisdiction covers "all persons, except for Israelis." Kontorovich (2021): "since 1993 the Palestinians have had their own government, which regulates almost every aspect of their lives."

The counter-rejoinder

Extending Israeli civil law personally and extraterritorially to one population — settlers — is precisely what occupation law does not require; a 58-year "temporary" occupation with state-encouraged settlement (Basic Law 2018 §7) strains the belligerent-occupation frame the rebuttal stands on.

What this page adjudicates

Nothing. It establishes the clauses. Whether the dual regime is occupation-law compliance or apartheid is the contested characterization — argued at the genealogy page, not here.

What the comparison shows — and what it cannot

A reading key, not a verdict

What it shows — kept to scope. A law is evidence only about the population it binds. On citizens citizens, the regional baseline is a religion-constituted order enforced against the state's own people: capital apostasy in five states, blasphemy statutes nearly everywhere, religion-gated office, worship confined to licensed buildings, every marriage code but Tunisia's voiding a Muslim woman's marriage out of the faith. Israel's citizen-scope instruments are of a different kind — a nation-state clause with no equality clause, land-allocation structures, religious personal-status courts (the shared millet inheritance) — alongside religious-practice freedom and anti-discrimination statutes without regional parallel. On immigration immigration, both sides discriminate openly — return for Jews, Muslim-only naturalization — in a domain where states far beyond this region sort entrants by ethnicity and religion; no clause in §II binds or burdens any existing citizen. On non-citizen residents residents, the region's hardest exclusions (Lebanon's Palestinians, the bidoon, the census Kurds) sit beside Israel's hardest: the West Bank dual regime and the family-unification bar. The three scopes do not convert into one another — an immigration preference is not a citizens-rights violation, and a clean citizens-rights record does not answer a resident-scope charge.

What it cannot show. A regional baseline does not adjudicate the apartheid claim. "Others discriminate too" is a tu-quoque — the charge against Israel stands or falls on the West Bank's legal architecture, not on Jordan's nationality law. Symmetrically: indicting Israel's legal order while passing over Jordan's art 3(2), Kuwait's art 4(5), Lebanon's Law 296, or Iran's art 881 bis is a selection effect, not an analysis. And the scope discipline cuts both ways: citing the Law of Return as proof of apartheid against Israel's citizens is the same category error as citing Egypt's apostasy doctrine to excuse the West Bank regime. The comparison's legitimate work is calibration — establishing the regional legal vocabulary, scope by scope, so that neither "uniquely racist state" nor "freest state in the region" is argued from an empty comparison set.

On-the-books is half the picture. Law-in-practice diverges in both directions: Qatar's capital apostasy clause has never been enforced; Saudi Arabia's church ban has no statute behind it yet is absolute in practice; Israel's equality statutes coexist with documented allocation gaps; Kuwait's constitution promises unrestricted belief over a Muslim-only citizenship. This page fixes the de-jure layer only — the layer the apartheid claim itself is pitched at.

This page documents what the laws say. It does not rank states or settle the characterization dispute — the clauses are the finding; the verdicts belong to the reader.

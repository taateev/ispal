Mutual perceptions across the divide

How the conflict’s publics perceive — and fail to perceive — one another: the recognition of the other side as a victim, and attitudes toward coexistence, exclusion and the state, read through a dehumanisation / “victim-illegibility” lens.

Compiled 2026-06-13 · Sources: PCPSR (Palestinian public, 2001 + 2023–25) · Pew Research Center Israel’s Religiously Divided Society (2016) · Smooha (1976–88 baseline) · Israeli attitudes to Goldstein (Teleseker 1994; Smith Institute 2023; IDI 2016; Sprinzak; Pedahzur & Perliger). Figures hand-verified against the source reports; OCR-uncertain values excluded. Self-contained SVG, no external libraries.

Read this as a method, not a verdict. Three axes are kept separate throughout — the operation (does the other register as a victim?), the speaker’s sincerity, and any claim’s truth-value. The page is deliberately bidirectional: showing only one direction would fail this project’s commitment to apply the same rigour to every actor. But — following Smooha (§05) — the directions are structurally asymmetric (a dominant majority vs a subordinate minority vs a population under blockade/occupation), so equal percentages do not mean equivalent acts. Hypotheses are flagged as hypotheses; gaps are named (§07).

01Palestinian → Israeli — the unrecognised victim

The direction we already had data for. Two PCPSR items show the same operation: the other side’s harm does not register as harm to a victim. When the victim is Israeli, the act is mostly not “terror” (Fig 1); and the October 7 killings are denied at ~90% — a denial the video evidence barely moves (Fig 2). This is the “illegibility of the other as victim” the companion thread studies.

02Israeli → Palestinian — the venerated perpetrator

The direct mirror to §01: where the other’s atrocity is denied, one’s own can be venerated. Days after Goldstein massacred 29 worshippers at prayer in 1994, Israelis condemned it overwhelmingly (78.8%) — but 3.6% praised him and 10.8% “understood” it against Arab terror (Fig 3). A Kahanist subculture then sainted the killer (the memorial volume Baruch Hagever; the Kiryat Arba grave-shrine). Twenty-nine years on, the veneration has grown and the tradition normalised: 10% of Jewish Israelis call Goldstein a “national hero,” 57% “a terrorist” and a third are unsure (Fig 4), and the party descended from his movement sits in cabinet. This is the symmetric counterpart to the companion thread’s Palestinian Hebron-1929 perpetrator-memory — a spectrum from condemnation to heroisation on both sides, unanimity on neither.

03Israeli-Jewish → Arab — exclusion, not illegibility

A second Israeli-side register, distinct again. A near-half of Israeli Jews endorse expelling or transferring Arabs (Fig 5) — an operation of majority-dominant removal, sharply graded by religiosity and politics, and paired with 79% who say Jews deserve preferential treatment and 97% who would be uncomfortable with a child marrying a Muslim. Surfacing this is not whataboutism; a one-directional page would fail the project’s apply-same-rigour rule. But removal ≠ the denial-of-victimhood in §01 — see §06.

04Israeli-Arab → the state — a legitimacy contest

Israeli Arab citizens’ attitudes run mostly against the ethnic character of the state, not toward dehumanising Jews. 64% say Israel cannot be both Jewish and democratic — a near-inversion of the Jewish 76% (Fig 6) — and 79% perceive “a lot” of discrimination (Fig 7). Pew has no direct Arab feeling-thermometer toward Jews; the closest proxy is marriage distance (82% of Muslims uncomfortable with a child marrying a Jew). Read these as a subordinate minority’s rejection of structured inequality, a distinct register again.

05A 1980s baseline, and why the dyads are not symmetric

Smooha’s 1976–88 series gives historical depth and the load-bearing caveat. His “ethnic democracy” concept — full citizenship for Arabs alongside institutionalised Jewish dominance — means the two attitude-directions operate from different structural positions (a dominant majority vs a subordinate minority), so they are not moral mirror images even when the percentages look comparable. All figures here are pre-Oslo.

06Hypotheses about the implications

Read across the registers, the data suggests several hypotheses (flagged as such — they are predictions, not findings): (H1) the directions are distinct operations, not one “mutual hatred” — victim-illegibility / asymmetric harm-labelling (Palestinian→Israeli), perpetrator-veneration (Israeli→Palestinian, the Goldstein register), majority-dominant removal (Jewish→Arab) and legitimacy-rejection (Arab→state) — and collapsing them destroys the structure that matters. Note the cleanest symmetry: denial of the other’s atrocity (§01) and veneration of one’s own perpetrator (§02) are the same coin, and both sides show the full spectrum. (H2) each is concentrated, not uniform: the Jewish expulsion attitude is religiosity-graded (Dati 71 vs Hiloni 36), just as PCPSR violence-support is region- and religiosity-graded (Gaza > West Bank; lower among Christians) — predicting the dehumanising register tracks sub-populations, not whole peoples. (H3) victim-illegibility should be strongest where a status-inversion prior is strongest (the companion thread’s dhimmi-prior), predicting it is more resistant to evidence than ordinary out-group hostility — consistent with Fig 2’s evidence-proof residue.

07What we cannot yet show — cross-checks to do

Honest gaps, named: (a) Gazans → Israeli Arab citizens — the dyad in the original request — has no clean survey source we could find; it remains a hypothesis (likely ambivalence: co-nationals who are also “normalised” with the enemy state). (b) Israeli Arabs → Jews directly: Pew offers no feeling-thermometer; we infer from state-legitimacy and social-distance items. (c) The three sources use different instruments (PCPSR’s terror/atrocity items; Pew’s expulsion/democracy items; Smooha’s index) — this page juxtaposes registers, it does not equate magnitudes. (d) All figures are majorities/spreads within heterogeneous publics, never unanimity. The next empirical step is comparable, same-instrument cross-party items and named-source instance collection.

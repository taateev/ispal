Mutual perceptions across the divide

How the conflict’s publics perceive — and fail to perceive — one another: the recognition of the other side as a victim, and attitudes toward coexistence, exclusion and the state, read through a dehumanisation / “victim-illegibility” lens.

Compiled 2026-06-13 · Sources: PCPSR (Palestinian public, 2001 + 2023–25) · Pew Research Center Israel’s Religiously Divided Society (2016) · Smooha (1976–88 baseline) · Israeli attitudes to Goldstein (Teleseker 1994; Smith Institute 2023; IDI 2016; Sprinzak; Pedahzur & Perliger). Every figure is shown with its full response distribution; cells a source did not publish are marked “—”, never invented. Self-contained SVG.

Read this as a method, not a verdict. Three axes are kept separate throughout — the operation (does the other register as a victim?), the speaker’s sincerity, and any claim’s truth-value. The page is deliberately bidirectional: showing only one direction would fail this project’s commitment to apply the same rigour to every actor. But — following Smooha (§05) — the directions are structurally asymmetric (a dominant majority vs a subordinate minority vs a population under blockade/occupation), so equal percentages do not mean equivalent acts. Hypotheses are flagged as hypotheses; gaps are named (§07).

01Palestinian → Israeli — the unrecognised victim

The direction we already had data for. The chemical/biological-weapons pair (Figs 1–2) is the cleanest single datapoint: the identical act is mostly “not terror” when the victim is Israeli and almost universally “terror” when the victim is Palestinian — and the full distributions show the intensity (Gaza’s “definitely not” on Q25 mirrors its “definitely yes” on Q26). The October 7 killings are then denied at ~90% (Figs 3–4), a denial only partly explained by never having seen the footage. This is the “illegibility of the other as victim” the companion thread studies.

02Israeli → Palestinian — the perpetrator-veneration register (read precisely, not as a tidy mirror)

Read this one carefully — it is not a 1:1 mirror of §01, and earlier framings here that called it “the same coin” were forcing a symmetry the data refuses. Baruch Goldstein was a lone aberration the Israeli mainstream condemned: 78.8% condemned the 1994 massacre (only 3.6% praised it, Fig 5); the state outlawed Kach within three days; in 2023, 57% of Jewish Israelis call him “a terrorist” vs 10% “a hero” (Fig 6). A Kahanist subculture saints him (the Baruch Hagever volume; the Kiryat Arba shrine), and that veneration is normalising — Ben-Gvir, who kept a Goldstein portrait, now sits in the cabinet. The Palestinian-side perpetrator-veneration that genuinely parallels this is not on this page (it is worked in the companion thread): the 1929-massacre perpetrators venerated as shuhada — but that is a mass communal massacre venerated by the mainstream, a different altitude from a lone shooter the mainstream condemns. The honest mass-event mirror to Palestinian non-condemnation of 1929 is Israeli non-condemnation of 1948 (Deir Yasin, the expulsions), not Goldstein. The mirrors sit at different altitudes; we do not force them.

03Israeli-Jewish → Arab — exclusion, not illegibility

A second Israeli-side register, distinct again. The full agree/disagree distribution on expelling or transferring Arabs (Fig 7) splits 48% agree / 46% disagree overall — an operation of majority-dominant removal, sharply graded by religiosity and politics. Surfacing this is not whataboutism; a one-directional page would fail the project’s apply-same-rigour rule. But removal ≠ the denial-of-victimhood in §01 — see §06.

04Israeli-Arab → the state — a legitimacy contest

Israeli Arab citizens’ attitudes run mostly against the ethnic character of the state, not toward dehumanising Jews. 64% say Israel cannot be both Jewish and democratic — a near-inversion of the Jewish 76% (Fig 8) — and 79% perceive “a lot” of discrimination (Fig 9). Pew has no direct Arab feeling-thermometer toward Jews; the closest proxy is marriage distance (82% of Muslims uncomfortable with a child marrying a Jew). Read these as a subordinate minority’s rejection of structured inequality, a distinct register again.

05A 1980s baseline, and why the dyads are not symmetric

Smooha’s 1976–88 series gives historical depth and the load-bearing caveat. His “ethnic democracy” concept — full citizenship for Arabs alongside institutionalised Jewish dominance — means the two attitude-directions operate from different structural positions (a dominant majority vs a subordinate minority), so they are not moral mirror images even when the percentages look comparable. All figures here are pre-Oslo (Fig 10).

06Hypotheses about the implications

Read across the registers, the data suggests several hypotheses (flagged as such — predictions, not findings): (H1) the directions are distinct operations, not one “mutual hatred” — victim-illegibility / asymmetric harm-labelling (Palestinian→Israeli), perpetrator-veneration (Israeli→Palestinian, the Goldstein register), majority-dominant removal (Jewish→Arab) and legitimacy-rejection (Arab→state) — and collapsing them destroys the structure that matters. (H1b) The mirrors sit at different altitudes — do not force them. Goldstein is a lone aberration the Israeli mainstream condemns; the Palestinian perpetrator-veneration that parallels it (the 1929 shuhada) is a mass event venerated by the mainstream — whose honest mirror is Israeli non-condemnation of 1948 (Deir Yasin, the expulsions), not Goldstein. Asymmetries run in several directions at once (condemnation-vs-veneration; structural power-to-enact; raw 1929 body counts) and none of them flatten to symmetry. (H2) each register is concentrated, not uniform: the Jewish expulsion attitude is religiosity-graded (Dati 71 vs Hiloni 36), just as PCPSR violence-support is region- and religiosity-graded (Gaza > West Bank; lower among Christians) — the dehumanising register tracks sub-populations, not whole peoples. (H3) victim-illegibility should be strongest where a status-inversion prior is strongest (the companion thread’s dhimmi-prior), predicting it is more resistant to evidence than ordinary out-group hostility — consistent with Fig 4’s evidence-proof residue.

07What we cannot yet show — cross-checks to do

Honest gaps, named: (a) Gazans → Israeli Arab citizens — the dyad in the original request — has no clean survey source we could find; it remains a hypothesis. (b) Israeli Arabs → Jews directly: Pew offers no feeling-thermometer; we infer from state-legitimacy and social-distance items. (c) Several published items withhold the full distribution (Q27’s 4-point worry scale; the discrimination scale’s lower three points; Poll 90’s “no/unsure” split; most Smooha Jewish items) — shown as “—”, never invented. (d) The sources use different instruments — this page juxtaposes registers, it does not equate magnitudes — and all figures are majorities/spreads within heterogeneous publics, never unanimity.

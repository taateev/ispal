Gazan demographic growth under three administrations, 1917–2008

Population, natural increase, mortality, fertility and life expectancy across British Mandate, Egyptian, and Israeli administration — with an Egypt benchmark.

Compiled 2026-06-06 · Sources: UNCTAD 1994; McCarthy 1990; Friedlander & Goldscheider 1979; Roy 1995/2016; Filiu 2014; World Bank WDI (PSE, EGY). Full provenance in gaza-demographic-growth-by-administration.md.

Master caveat. Birth/death registration in Gaza was incomplete before 1967, and deaths were under-registered worse than births under both the Egyptian and the early Israeli administrations — so recorded crude death rates run too low and recorded natural increase too high. Recorded-vs-corrected is shown where sources allow. "Muslim" = all-Palestine Muslim benchmark (no Gaza-only series exists for that cell). On "West Bank + Gaza": the continuous modern lines use the World Bank / UN series, which publish only the combined territory — neither breaks out Gaza. PCBS does report Gaza separately, but as periodic benchmarks (3-year averages, census years), not a clean annual series. Those benchmarks show Gaza ≈ West Bank on life expectancy (so the combined line ≈ Gaza), slightly worse on infant/child mortality (2014: Gaza IMR 19.6 vs WB 17.1; under-5 23.7 vs 20.0), and notably higher on fertility (2011–13: Gaza TFR 4.5 vs WB 3.7) — so combined slightly understates Gaza's mortality and understates its fertility. The Gaza-only charts (1, 3, 14) and all pre-1990 figures use Gaza-specific data.

DensityPopulation density

All of Gaza's ~9× growth since 1948 was absorbed into a fixed ~365 km² (141 sq mi) — the borders never moved. So density tracks population exactly: from ~670 to ~6,100 people/km².

Context — the average understates built-up density. Roy records the 1948 refugee influx raising density from ~500/sq mi (1944) to ~2,300/sq mi (1948); densities within the camps run higher — Jabalya ≈ 133,400/sq mi (~51,500/km²). For scale, Manhattan ≈ 28,000/km²; Gaza's Strip-wide ~6,100/km² (2023) is a dense-city figure averaged across the whole territory (including farmland), so where people actually live is denser than that.

Camps are denser still. Roy's camp-vs-non-camp split (1993): refugee camps ≈ 197,070/sq mi (~76,000/km²) vs non-camp ≈ 20,500/sq mi (~7,900/km²) — camps 9.6× the non-camp figure; Jabalya ≈ 133,400/sq mi (~51,500/km²). Camps held 28.7% of the population on 7.2% of built-up land (1986). So measured density across the Strip ranges from ~3,400/km² (Khan Yunis governorate) to ~76,000/km² (camps) — a ~22× range the single average doesn't show.

Land use. Most of the non-built-up Strip is agricultural, not vacant. Roughly half the Strip (~180 of 365 km²) was cultivated in the mid-20th century (Roy); agricultural land has since fallen to ~100 km² (ARIJ/FAO) as urban area expanded (~6 km²/yr, 2002–13). This farmland is also why the Strip-wide average density understates the built-up figure: the population is spread statistically across cropland it doesn't live on. (Land use here is a measurement and land-cover point, not a claim about crowding.)

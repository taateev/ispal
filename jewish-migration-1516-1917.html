Across the four centuries of Ottoman rule, Jewish in-migration into Palestine ran through three principal channels — the post-Expulsion Sephardi founding of Safed and the holy cities and its kabbalistic golden age, religious and messianic migration (the Hasidic aliyot, the Lithuanian Perushim, the Yemenite and Bukharian streams) sustained by the halukkah charity network, and refugee flight from the Russian pogroms (the First and Second Aliyot). This table lists each documented cohort with its period, an order-of-magnitude population estimate, its legal status and land tenure, its principal reception zones, and the kind of evidence on which the entry rests.

On legal status & land tenure Jewish immigrants received no muhajir-style package. The Old Yishuv lived as dhimmi protected subjects in the holy-city quarters; from the 1830s many gained European consular protection under the Capitulations — especially after the 1838 British consulate in Jerusalem — which eased protected immigration. From 1881 the Hamidian government moved the other way: the “red ticket” curbs restricted Jewish entry and barred land purchase, so First- and Second-Aliyah settlers circumvented them through the capitulations and bought land privately via the Rothschild administration and the JCA / JNF, registered to Ottoman subjects — the structural mirror-image of the muhajir land grants on the Muslim sheet.

Iberian Sefardim

The largest early-Ottoman Jewish stream: exiles of the 1492 Spanish Expulsion and their descendants, arriving via Salonika, Istanbul and Italy. They turned Safed into a kabbalistic centre and reinforced Jerusalem, Hebron and Tiberias.

02 16th c.Safed congregations
Italian, Maghrebi & Ashkenazi Sefardim

Alongside the Iberian majority, the Safed tax registers name distinct congregations — Italian-peninsula (Apulia, Calabria), North-African Maghrebi, and German-Hungarian Ashkenazi — each a small chain-migration cohort within the city's sixteenth-century boom.

03 1563–1564Tiberias project
Don Joseph Nasi's Tiberias project

A state-sponsored venture: the Sultan granted Don Joseph Nasi the ruined Tiberias, whose walls were rebuilt for a planned silk colony for Jewish refugees. The scheme faded after Nasi's death and was demographically minor, but it is the era's clearest charter-backed settlement attempt.

 Charter project rebuilt Tiberias walls; faded after Nasi's death Sultanic charter state concession of Tiberias — the era's one land grant Tiberias sultanic grant Chronicle Secondary
04 16th–18th c.Continuous baseline
Sephardi pilgrimage-into-settlement

The demographically dominant stream: a continuous pilgrimage-into-settlement of Sephardi and Mizrahi Jews from across the Ottoman world into the four holy cities, sustained from the sixteenth century onward. It made up some 80 per cent of the Old Yishuv.

05 1666–1700Sabbatean networks
Sabbatean migrants

In the wake of Shabbetai Tzvi's 1666 messianic movement, clandestine and then open Sabbatean migrants from Italy, Smyrna and the Polish-Lithuanian networks settled in Jerusalem and Hebron, forming the substrate for Judah he-Hasid's 1700 wave.

 Network-scale substrate for the 1700 wave Dhimmi subjects clandestine; no special tenure Jerusalem · Hebron from Italy, Smyrna, Poland-Lithuania Testimony Secondary

06 1700Sabbatean wave
R. Judah he-Hasid's wave

A wave of roughly 1,500 Polish and Lithuanian Jews, many from Sabbatean-influenced networks, set out for Jerusalem under R. Judah he-Hasid in 1700. Many died en route; the Ashkenazi courtyard they founded was burned by creditors in 1720, leaving the ruin long known as the Hurva.

R. Hayyim Abulafia of Izmir and his students re-established the Jewish community of Tiberias in 1740 under the protection of Ẓāhir al-ʿUmar, rebuilding the quarter the Zaydani ruler invited them to repopulate.

09 1747–1777Hasidic aliyot
Hasidic aliyot

Successive Hasidic groups from Galicia, Ukraine and Belarus settled in the holy cities across the later eighteenth century, culminating in the "Great Aliyah" of roughly 300 Hasidim under R. Menahem-Mendel of Vitebsk in 1777, who settled first in Safed and then Tiberias.

Disciples of the Vilna Gaon — the Perushim — emigrated from Lithuania in 1809–1812 and re-established an Ashkenazi presence in Jerusalem and Safed.

Habad Hasidim

Habad Hasidim from Belarus settled in Hebron from 1816, founding the community that anchored Habad's presence in the city.

12 1800–1882Protected immigration
Old Yishuv — protected European immigration

The Old Yishuv's rise from about 5,000 in 1800 to roughly 24,000 by 1882 was driven by substantial European-Jewish in-migration — an estimated 25,000–45,000 cumulative protected arrivals between 1838 and 1882, well above the net rise, since high mortality and return-migration offset much of the inflow. It was sustained by the halukkah charity network and the Pekidim-ve-Amarkalim institutions and enabled by European consular protection after 1838.

13 1882–1903First Aliyah
First Aliyah (Russian-pogrom + Sephardic)

The First Aliyah brought predominantly refugees of the Russian pogroms together with a substantial Sephardic minority — perhaps a third were Sephardi/Mizrahi, and only a small core was ideologically Zionist. They founded the moshavot — Petah Tikva (1878), Rishon le-Zion (1882) — and settled urban quarters.

14 1881+Yemenite
Yemenite Jews

Yemenite Jews began arriving from 1881, drawn by religious and messianic expectation rather than political Zionism, settling in Jerusalem, Hebron and the Galilee at an average of about 184 a year before the First World War.

Bukharian, Persian & Iraqi Jews

Bukharian, Persian and Iraqi Jews migrated from the 1880s, religiously and economically motivated; the Bukharian community founded its own quarter in Jerusalem in 1891, substantial by the 1900s.

16 1904–1914Second Aliyah
Second Aliyah

The Second Aliyah came mostly from the Russian Pale after the 1905 pogroms; though it furnished the labour-Zionist vanguard and the first kibbutz precursors, the great majority — 70 to 80 per cent — eventually left the country (yerida).

Note on method & sources

This is a descriptive ledger, not an argument, and a companion to the Muslim and Christian in-migration sheets for the full Ottoman period. Each row records a distinct stream of Jewish settlement into Palestine, ordered by approximate start date and grouped into three eras. The source-tradition tags indicate the kind of evidence behind each entry — Hebrew narrative chronicles, first-person emigration letters and memoir, the halukkah and Pekidim-ve-Amarkalim communal registers, European consular records, and Ottoman tax registers and immigration statistics. Entries resting only on modern synthesis carry a Secondary tag.

The classical centuries are dominated by the post-Expulsion Sephardi founding. The 1492 exiles and their descendants made Safed a kabbalistic centre and anchored the four holy cities; the named Ashkenazi waves (Judah he-Hasid, the Hasidim, the Perushim) were smaller religious cohorts layered onto that continuous Sephardi/Mizrahi baseline. The shift to mass flow begins in the Tanzimat decades, not only at 1881: the Old Yishuv's protected European immigration of 1838–82 — a gross inflow of some 25,000–45,000, far above its net growth — already marks the rupture, which the First and Second Aliyot then carry forward. It parallels the Egyptian and refugee flows opening on the Muslim sheet from 1831.

Population figures are order-of-magnitude only, though the Jewish-side figures are generally firmer than their Muslim counterparts, resting on Ottoman tax registers, communal counts, consular protection lists and registered immigration. The window opens at 1516 with the Ottoman conquest and closes at 1917 with the end of Ottoman rule, on the eve of the Mandate.

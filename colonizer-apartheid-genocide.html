"My readers have a general idea of the history of colonization in other countries… Native populations, civilized or uncivilized, have always stubbornly resisted the settlers." — Vladimir Jabotinsky, "The Iron Wall," 1923 — using the word as the movement's own. The valence flipped only after 1960.

"Colonizer," "apartheid," and "genocide" are usually heard as one undifferentiated charge. They are not. They entered the argument at different times, by different people, in different registers — and each escalates the one before it in legal weight: colonizer (a structural characterization) → apartheid (a crime defined in 1973) → genocide (the 1948 "crime of crimes"). This page lays the three side by side as a dated genealogy. Two threads run through all of it: the word "colonization" was Zionism's own, neutral-to-proud, for the movement's first half-century; and South Africa is the hinge that recurs in every strand. Each framing also carries a live ↺ rebuttal track, marked here in the same pass — the empirical test of whether an argument is "resistant to counter-evidence."

I · Roots — when "colonization" was the movement's own word · 1882–1945

Before it was an indictment, "colonization" was a badge. The moshavot were "colonies"; the movement's bank and land funds wore the word. The valence inverts only after global decolonization (c. 1960). The Armenian genocide, meanwhile, enters the discourse first as a Jewish self-reference — fear of the fate awaiting Jews — not as an accusation against Zionism.

II · The frame becomes an indictment · 1948–1979

After 1948 the vocabulary turns. The settler-colonial thesis is built first in Palestinian-political and French-Marxist circles — for the specifically Israeli case — a generation before the academic theory. The apartheid comparison is born on the apartheid side. And "extermination" enters first as an Arab threat of war, not an accusation — a vector worth keeping straight.

III · Academic institutionalization · 1982–2012

The frames move from polemic to social science, law, and inquiry — and the rebuttal track sharpens alongside them: the only UN resolution ever rescinded, a Nobel jurist's refutation, a Holocaust historian's controlled "no."

IV · Mainstreaming & the genocide turn · 2014–2025

The apartheid frame reaches the major human-rights institutions; the genocide frame moves from isolated scholarship to the International Court of Justice. The rebuttal track moves with it — a book-length refutation, a concept-critique from within genocide studies, a charge that the settler-colonial paradigm is itself unfalsifiable.

The word "genocide" has four registers

The single label hides four different vectors. Conflating them — reading an epithet as a court brief, or dismissing a scholarly argument as an epithet — is the error on every side. These mark vector and venue (who, at whom, in what setting) — not a ranking of legitimacy or truth.

R1 · 1915+
Self-referential
The Armenian fate invoked by Jews about Jews (Aaronsohn). Not an accusation against Zionism — and the dominant pre-1948 register.
R2 · 1948
Wartime threat
Azzam Pasha's "war of extermination" — vector points at the Jews. The opposite of the modern frame.
R3 · 1948–70s
Accusation (epithet)
Deir Yassin-as-genocide; Shukeiri's "Eichmann in a state"; the Soviet–Arab "Zionism = racism" push. The real pre-1982 accusation layer.
R4 · 2010+
Academic argument
Shaw, Moses, Albanese — a Convention/Lemkinian concept argued with stated criteria, exposed to refutation (Bartov).

How to read these framings

A reading key, not a verdict

The three are an escalation ladder, not one charge. They arrive at different times and carry different legal weight; collapsing "colonizer / apartheid / genocide" into a single bloc is itself an analytic error the genealogy corrects.

Each strand has a live rebuttal track — the 1991 revocation, Goldstone, Pogrund, Bartov, Moses, Kirsch. That is the empirical test of "resistant to counter-evidence": a frame whose proponents cannot say what would change their minds fits the charge; one in vigorous internal contest does not. The same falsifiability test applies to the counter-frame — closure cuts both ways.

The honest unit is the claim, not the speaker. There is no rhetoric-free zone to stand on — scholarly prose is its own rhetoric of objectivity, and "genocide" is a loaded word whichever register uses it. What survives is a narrow, defeasible test, run per claim and symmetrically: can this specific statement be checked against evidence both sides would recognize — a document, a date, a quoted line? That certifies nothing as non-rhetorical; it only sorts what can be tested from what cannot. It is why every entry above is tagged by evidence level, not by who said it.

This page documents the history of an argument — it does not endorse or refute any of the three framings. The genealogy is the finding; the verdicts belong to the reader.

The Jewish community of Hebron is one of the oldest claims in the country — and one of the most intermittent in the record. The corpus documents a synagogue at the Cave of Machpelah from the Muslim conquest (c.638), a named Geniza-era community in the 11th–13th centuries, the rupture of the Crusader conquest (1099), a roughly 330-year stretch with no named Hebron Jew, and a Sephardi/kabbalist re-founding from c.1540. Two registers are kept distinct: institutional continuity (the synagogue) and prosopographic continuity (named people) — they do not coincide. Click any row to read the underlying source passage.

Hebron's Jews · households & the record

A tiny community traced across nine centuries: present-but-uncounted under early Islam, named in the Geniza, broken by the Crusades, absent by name through the Mamluk centuries, and re-founded by Sephardi exiles — compiled from a multi-source corpus.

Jewish households & the documented record, by year

Not a growth curve — a tiny, repeatedly-interrupted community. Present-but-uncounted under early Islam (dashed), reduced to near-zero after the Crusader conquest, absent by name across the Mamluk centuries, and never more than ~20 households even at its 15th–16th-century revival.

The dashed pre-1170 curve is an estimate — the early-Islamic synagogue community is attested but never counted; solid points are documented (Benjamin 1170; the 1480s travelers; the 1538–1596 Ottoman defters). Unit: tax-paying households (hāne). The shaded band is the Mamluk hollow — the ~270 years in which no Hebron Jew is named at all.

Reading the record. Three findings the corpus corrects against the conventional narrative. (1) The 638 synagogue is a tradition, preserved in a 12th-century Christian manuscript, not a contemporary 7th-c. document — institutional evidence, not a named individual. (2) 1099 at Hebron was a capture, not a massacre: Godfrey of Bouillon took it and held it as a lordship; the famous storming- massacre was at Jerusalem, and no Hebron-Jewish massacre account survives. (3) Named Hebron Jews exist in two windows only — the 11th–13th-c. Geniza community (Saadia the ḥaver and three others) and the post-1540 Sephardi revival — bracketing a ~330-year gap with no named Hebron Jew of any origin.

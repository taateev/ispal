One question runs through this sheet: how did each community of Palestine grow — and under which government? The Arab-Muslim population is the demographic body of the country and the focus here; it is tracked alongside the Arab-Christian, Jewish (decomposed by origin and aliyah), Druze, Samaritan, and non-Arab Christian communities. The frame is regime by regime — late Ottoman, British Mandate, the 1948 rupture, the 1948–67 three-way split, Israeli occupation, and the Palestinian Authority — because the single largest fact about this population is that it grew at very different rates under different governments, and that 1948 was a displacement, not a demographic event. Every figure carries its evidentiary character: Ottoman register, census, official statistics, modern synthesis, or estimate.

The whole-territory ledger, 1878–1946

While Palestine was a single territory, the authoritative count is Justin McCarthy, The Population of Palestine (1990) — the Ottoman registers and the three Mandate censuses, corrected for undercount, by religion. Ottoman figures are Ottoman citizens: they undercount foreign-passport Jews (most First- and Second-Aliyah olim held foreign nationality), so the 1914 Jewish total is ~39,000 citizens but ~60,000 residents. The Christian column is little affected by the citizen distinction.

Census raw totals run below McCarthy’s corrected figures above (1922 Muslims 590,890 raw vs. 638,407 corrected) because Ottoman and British counts under-recorded women, infants, and — above all — the Negev Bedouin, who “would not agree to be counted” and were carried as a flat ~55,000 estimate. Every Muslim figure on this sheet is therefore a floor.

1948 — the rupture

Read this first — a displacement, not a demographic event The drop in the Arab population of the territory between 1946 and 1950 is not births and deaths — it is flight and expulsion. Of ~1.3 million Arabs in Mandatory Palestine, ~700,000–750,000 were displaced in 1947–49; roughly 156,000 remained inside the new state of Israel. The refugees did not vanish from the count — most reappear in the West Bank and Gaza tallies below (and in Jordan, Lebanon, Syria). Where the lines on this sheet jump or drop at 1948 and again at 1967, that is people moving, and it is marked, never smoothed.

Three regimes, 1948–1967 — one people, three governments

For nineteen years the Palestinian Arab population lived under three separate states, each with its own statistics and its own demographic regime: a Jewish-majority Israel, the Jordanian-annexed West Bank, and the Egyptian-administered Gaza Strip. The contrast is the cleanest natural experiment on the sheet — same people, different governments, different growth.

Why the West Bank grew so slowly under Jordan. Fertility was just as high there as in Gaza, but the West Bank had an open hinterland — Jordanians could move east to Amman and the East Bank for work — so net growth ran only ~0.5–1.4%/yr. Gaza, sealed against Egypt, had nowhere to send its surplus, and so its registered population climbed ~3%/yr despite stagnant mortality (life expectancy stuck near 48–50 across the whole Egyptian period). The 1948 refugee shock — Gaza’s population tripled in months — is displacement, kept distinct from this natural growth.

Occupation & Authority, 1967–present

In June 1967 Israel took the West Bank and Gaza; the immediate census drops below are war flight (~250–300K from the West Bank to the East Bank; ~90–100K from Gaza), not slowed growth. Then came the demographic signature of the occupation: Israeli health infrastructure collapsed mortality — Gaza’s life expectancy rose ~14 years in 15 — while fertility stayed very high, driving natural increase toward ~4%/yr. After 1995 the Palestinian Authority’s own bureau (PCBS) takes over the count, and fertility at last begins its decline.

Recombine the three pieces and the territory holds today ~7.2M Jews and ~7.3M Arabs (Muslim ~7.1M, Christian ~0.2M, Druze ~0.15M) — rough demographic parity, the headline number of the whole sheet, reached along three different statistical paths under five different governments.

Arab-Muslim growth, regime by regime

This is the question the sheet is built around. Population growth is natural increase (births minus deaths) plus or minus migration. The Arab-Muslim population grew by natural increase in every period — but the realized rate swung from ~1% to nearly 4% a year, and the swing tracks the government: what it did to mortality (health infrastructure) and what it allowed for migration (an open or sealed hinterland). 1948 itself is absent here — it was displacement, not growth.

The pattern: the Jordanian West Bank is the low outlier — identical fertility to Gaza, but an open border bled its growth into Amman. Israeli Arabs are the high outlier — ~3.8%/yr for three-quarters of a century, almost pure natural increase with negligible migration, among the fastest sustained rates ever recorded for any population. The occupation-era West Bank and Gaza peak when Israeli health infrastructure crushed infant mortality while fertility stayed high. Only under the Palestinian Authority does the rate finally bend down, as fertility falls — fastest in the West Bank, slowest in Gaza.

The inputs — fertility, mortality, and the engine of growth

Growth is a subtraction: births minus deaths. The Arab population grew so fast because, for decades, that subtraction stayed wide — fertility held near its biological maximum while mortality fell away beneath it. This section opens the engine. The natural comparators are Jordan and Egypt — the states that administered the West Bank and Gaza from 1948 to 1967, and the region's two demographic baselines. They ran the same transition; the Palestinian populations are its laggards, Gaza most of all.

The mechanism. Two mortality declines bracket the story — a Mandate-era halving of infant deaths (Muslim IMR 199→91, 1924–46) under British anti-malaria and public-health work, then a stall through the Egyptian period in Gaza (life expectancy ~48–50, infant mortality ~120–150, 1948–67), then a steep post-1967 collapse under occupation (Gaza life expectancy +14 years in fifteen). Fertility barely moved until the 1990s — so natural increase stayed at ~3–3.7%/yr. On the timing of the fertility decline the Palestinian populations are the regional laggards: Egypt, Jordan and Israeli Arabs are now near replacement (~2.6–2.9), while Gaza was still ~3.9 as late as 2017–19 — the last of the region to begin falling, which is exactly why its growth stayed exceptional. *West Bank & Gaza maternal mortality is reported jointly (~16 per 100,000, 2023). The October-2023 war sharply reversed Gaza's mortality figures — a shock, not a trend; not plotted here.

Where they lived — population density by region

The people of Palestine were never spread evenly across it. The late-Ottoman map is a steep gradient: a densely terraced highland spine — Hebron, Jerusalem, Nablus — carrying ~25 people per km²; a thinly-settled coastal plain and inland valleys at ~8–10, much of it malarial swamp; and a near-empty Negev, half the country by area, at ~4. That uneven map is the hinge of the settlement story: the highland villages were peasant-owned and not for sale, while the thin lowland zones held the large absentee estates that were — so Zionist land purchase concentrated there, in the under-settled coast, Jezreel and Galilee lowlands (Grossman). For the coast reconstructed town by town, see Population of the Palestine coast, 1800–1914.

Grossman's verdict on the lowland zone is blunt — "a density of eight to ten persons per square kilometre is undeniably under-settled land." Palestine-wide, the average climbed as the population grew: ~9/km² at the 1596 tax register, ~18 by 1872, then 29 (1922) → 39 (1931) → 75 (1947) across the Mandate — the same growth the charts above track, now read against the land.

Population density today, 2023

People per km². The one place the old map inverted: the Gaza coast, ~9/km² in 1870, is now among the densest territories on earth — ~200,000 refugees packed into 365 km² after 1948, then generations of the region's highest natural increase.

The inversion is the displacement story written into the land: the thinly-settled southern coast of the nineteenth century became ~11× the density of the West Bank — the historically dense highland — because the 1948 refugees had nowhere else to go. Densities use pre-war area and population; the Negev pulls Israel's national average (~445/km²) far below its coastal core, where most Israelis actually live.

The communities, one by one

Jewish — by origin and aliyah

The Jewish curve is the one driven by immigration, not natural increase — and its origin mix inverts twice. The Old Yishuv of 1878 was Sephardi/Mizrahi-majority and Ottoman-subject; European religious immigration made it Ashkenazi-majority by 1914. Then the great waves — overwhelmingly European — built the Yishuv, though arrivals badly overstate residents (yerida ran 50–80% in the early waves). After 1948 the mass aliyah of ~700,000–800,000 Jews from Arab and North-African lands turned a European-majority society roughly even by 1961; the post-1990 Soviet wave (~1M) tilted it back; and the Israeli-born “sabra” share climbed to ~78% by 2023.

Israeli Jews by continent of birth: Israel-born ~35% (1948) → 46% (1970) → ~78% (2023); the Asia–Africa (Mizrahi) and Europe–America shares each near ~30% after the 1948–60 wave, the European share rising again with the FSU influx. Arab Christian — the share that fell while the number rose

Christianity in Palestine rose across the late Ottoman period in both number and share — ~27,000 / 7.9% (1850) → ~81,000 / 11.2% (1914) — the Tanzimat-era recovery of urban, great-power-patronised Christian communities. The absolute number kept climbing through the Mandate to ~149,000 (1946), but the share fell to 7.7% because the Jewish denominator exploded. The absolute collapse comes only after 1948: Nakba emigration halved the count, and steady out-migration from Bethlehem and Ramallah has kept the West Bank Christian total flat (~40–50,000) ever since, while the Muslim total around it grew ~×4½. Today Christians are ~1.5% of the West Bank, ~0.05% of Gaza, and rising only inside Israel (~145,000 Arab Christians; ~185,000 counting non-Arab Christians).

Druze

Counted discretely only in 1922 (7,028) and 1931 (~9,148); Ben-Gurion’s November-1948 diary gives “18 villages, approximately 12,000 souls.” The community held full Israeli citizenship and compulsory IDF conscription from 1956, and grew to ~150,000 by 2023 — but that figure is not continuous with the Mandate count: it absorbs the ~25,000 Golan Druze who came under Israeli control only in 1967. The genuine Galilee-and-Carmel continuation is closer to ~125,000.

Samaritan — collapse, then recovery

The clearest case of an indigenous community ground to near-extinction: from ~1,400 in the 16th-century tax registers (Nablus, Gaza, Damascus, Cairo) to a single endogamous remnant of ~140–150 at Nablus by 1900 — the lowest point in their recorded history — by dhimmi extraction, forced-conversion ratchets, and a documented 1841 extirpation crisis survived only by the intervention of the Sephardi Chief Rabbi of Jerusalem. The 1922 census counted 163; the 1931, ~182. The 20th-century recovery — American philanthropy from ~1900, then state protection — has brought them to ~800 today, split between Kiryat Luza on Mount Gerizim (West Bank) and Holon (Israel): one community straddling two regimes.

Non-Arab Christians — Templers, Armenians, the foreign communities

Distinct from the indigenous Arab Christians, the censuses also held European and other foreign Christians, never broken out by nationality. The German Templers (Württemberg Pietists) settled six colonies — Haifa, Jaffa, Sarona, Wilhelma, Jerusalem, Bethlehem-of-Galilee, all inside the future Israel — peaking at ~800 settlers pre-WWI and ~2,200 by the interwar years, before the British interned and expelled them as enemy aliens in 1939–48; Sarona is today the HaKirya government complex in Tel Aviv. The Armenians of the Jerusalem Armenian Quarter, swelled by survivors of the 1915 genocide, were subsumed in the “Christian” total (no discrete census count; rough estimates ~3,000–15,000 interwar, ~1,000–2,000 today). Russian Orthodox, Greek and Latin clergy, and other Europeans make up the rest — and the 1,862 Hindus and Sikhs of 1922 were the British-Indian Army garrison, not settlers.

Note on method & sources

The series breaks at 1948, and the break is the point. Through 1946 this is a single territory with a single authoritative count (McCarthy, corrected). After 1948 it is three territories under three governments; the “whole-territory” figures from 1950 on are the three pieces recombined, and are approximate sums across benchmark years that do not perfectly align. The chart bridges the gap with a dashed line precisely so the discontinuity is visible rather than smoothed.

Three things are kept analytically separate. (1) Displacement — the ~700,000 refugees of 1948 and the ~350,000 of 1967 — from natural demographic change. (2) Arrivals (gross immigration) from resident population (yerida removed most early olim). (3) Citizens from residents (the Ottoman Jewish count excludes foreign-passport olim). Each is flagged where it bites.

Known soft spots. The Negev Bedouin were never enumerated — a flat ~55,000 estimate — so every Muslim figure is a floor. The Egyptian-period (1948–67) Gaza vital series barely exists; deaths were under-registered under both Egyptian and early-Israeli administration, so recorded growth runs high. Post-1990 West Bank/Gaza religion splits are thin (PCBS publishes little by religion). Modern Druze and Samaritan counts, and all post-1990 totals, rest on official statistics, not corpus primaries. The world-average growth line is period-binned from UN DESA / Our World in Data; the 2023–26 Arab figures are an estimate (war deaths per Gaza Ministry of Health and Lancet ranges, emigration, and reduced births), shown dashed and carrying wide error bars.

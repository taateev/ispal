"We must take the longer and more difficult path — the path toward the Arab worker… only an alliance of Jewish and Arab workers will establish and maintain an alliance of the Jewish and Arab peoples in Palestine." — David Ben-Gurion, Aḥdut Ha‘avoda congress, Ein Harod, May 1924 (Lockman, Comrades and Enemies). The same Ben-Gurion was the architect of Hebrew Labor / Avoda Ivrit, the campaign to replace Arab workers with Jews. Both were his — and the data below is the gap between the aspiration and the institution.

Two facts sit awkwardly together. Hebrew Labor — the drive to staff the Jewish economy with Jews only — was real, organized, and central to Labor Zionism. And yet through the whole inter-war period Jews and Arabs worked side by side in large numbers, the campaign largely failed on its own terms until 1936, and Jewish-led efforts to build joint Arab–Jewish unions ran alongside the displacement drive the entire time. This page holds both: the numbers of cross-employment (where the famous "35%" turns out to mean three different things), and the institutions — four competing streams of labor politics, only some of them Zionist, and what defeated the cross-communal ones. Figures are benchmark points, not a continuous series; the densest annual data exists only for Haifa, and is treated separately as such.

The number everyone cites means three different things

"Arab labor was still ~35%" is true — of Jewish agriculture, at the beginning of 1936. Across the whole Jewish economy the share was far lower and falling. Why the gap. Arab labor in the Jewish sector was concentrated in agriculture — in 1935, 7,000 of the 12,000 Arabs employed by Jews were in "mostly citrus" agriculture (Metzer). So within that sector the share ran ~35%, while spread across the whole Jewish economy — by the mid-1930s dominated by urban Jewish immigrant labor — it was about one in ten. Both numbers are correct; they answer different questions. Treating the within-agriculture 35% as the economy-wide figure overstates Arab presence by roughly two-to-three-fold.

The share fell while the head-count rose

The two movements look contradictory and aren't. The Fifth-Aliya immigration boom (1932–35) swelled the Jewish labor force far faster than Arab employment could be pushed out — so the Arab share dropped even as the absolute number of Arabs working for Jews climbed to an all-time peak in 1935.

Six findings

01
"Still 35% Arab" is a sectoral figure wearing an economy-wide costume
The ~35% is the Arab share of Jewish agriculture at the start of 1936. Economy-wide the share was ~10% of all persons, ~15–20% of wage earners. The three numbers diverge because Arab labor clustered in citrus farming. Quoting the agricultural figure for the whole economy is the single most common error here.
02
Share and head-count moved in opposite directions — both real
From 1931 to 1935 the absolute number of Arabs employed by Jews rose 7,000 → 12,000 (a peak), while their share of the Jewish economy fell. The Jewish labor force was growing at 14.6% a year against the Arab labor force's 1.9%. The boom pulled Arab workers in faster than Hebrew Labor pushed them out.
03
Hebrew Labor largely failed until the Revolt did its work for it
After three decades of campaigning, Arab labor was still ~35% of Jewish agriculture in 1936. Anita Shapira titled her book on the campaign The Futile Struggle; Metzer concludes "the unskilled labor market was far from segregated." Economic separation arrived not by doctrine but by the 1936–39 Arab Revolt boycott — Arab workers withdrew, and wartime substitution made it stick.
04
There were genuine cross-communal alternatives — defeated internally by 1927
Four labor-politics streams competed: the Hebrew Labor mainstream, the anti-Zionist Communist Party's joint organizing, the Brit Shalom binationalists, and operational joint workforces in British industries. The maximal Labor-Zionist reach-out (Ben-Gurion's 1924 "path toward the Arab worker," Kaplansky's binational parliament) lost the internal fight at the 1927 Histadrut congress — before the 1929 riots, which then removed the remaining rationale.
05
The market was segmented, not segregated — a wage ceiling, not a wall
Arab and Jewish workers met constantly, but at structurally different wages: in 1932 an unskilled urban European Jew earned a 250-mil minimum, a town Arab 100–120, a rural Arab 80. The abundant supply of cheap Arab (and Hourani migrant) labor set an effective ceiling on unskilled Jewish wages — which is the economic engine that produced Hebrew Labor in the first place (Shafir's matched-wage-competition origin).
06
Only Haifa has continuous annual data — and the government sector stayed ~90% Arab
National figures are benchmark snapshots; Bernstein's Haifa micro-history is the one place with year-by-year series. There the British-run railways and port stayed overwhelmingly Arab (railway Jews 8–13% across 1933–37); the sharp Judaization that did occur — Haifa port Jews 10% (1934) → 39% (1937), citrus porterage handed to Jewish labor — happened during and after the Revolt, not before.

Four streams of labor politics

Within the Yishuv the displacement-oriented mainstream competed with at least three Jewish-led streams that tried to build cross-communal organization. Hebrew Labor consolidated its dominance only after these alternatives were politically defeated — not from the start (Lockman, Bernstein, Cohen Year Zero).

Hebrew Labor mainstream
Aḥdut Ha‘avoda → Mapai · Histadrut · 1905+
Jewish self-labor + displacement of Arab labor from Jewish enterprises. The "separate but equal" model: cooperate on wages, bar Arabs from full Histadrut membership.
→ consolidated dominance after 1929
Palestine Communist Party (PKP)
anti-Zionist · 1923–29 core
"Jewish and Arab workers unite" — the genuinely egalitarian joint-organizing, but explicitly anti-Zionist. Jewish leadership Arabized under Comintern pressure after 1929.
→ defeated 1929; persisted marginal to 1944
Brit Shalom binationalists
Magnes · Buber · Scholem · 1925–33
Zionist-binationalist intellectuals favoring a unitary state of equals. Not labor organizers as such; dissolved after Hebron 1929.
→ dissolved by 1933
Operational joint workforces
Haifa rail / port / IPC · citrus · 1906–48
Day-to-day cooperation in British-administered and private industries; repeated joint strikes (1932 lightermen, 1935–36 IPC) despite Histadrut ambivalence.
→ persisted, politically isolated, to 1948

The wage structure underneath it all

Hebrew Labor was a response to a wage gap, not its cause. The 1932 government Labor Legislation Committee's minimum daily wage tiers show the split labor market directly — and that unskilled Oriental (Mizrahi) Jews earned the same as Arabs, marking their peripheral position in the European-led labor movement.

Minimum daily wage by worker category, 1932 (mils/day)
government Labor Legislation Committee tiers · source: Bernstein, Constructing Boundaries
And it held into the 1940s, skilled work included. In 1943 a Jewish carpenter earned 764 mils/day against an Arab carpenter's 425; a Jewish machine printer 900 vs 395; a Jewish fitter 836 vs 525 (Bernstein, from Gertz). The supply of cheap Arab and Hourani migrant labor — Vashitz put non-Palestinian Arab workers at 25,000–30,000 in 1934, ~25% of Arab wage labor — imposed an effective ceiling on unskilled Jewish wages (Sussman). That ceiling, not ideology alone, is what made the displacement campaign economically intelligible.

Haifa · the one place with annual data

National cross-employment is census-benchmarked; Bernstein's Haifa study is the only continuous year-by-year window. It is one city, and the port and railways were government-owned mixed workplaces — so "Jewish share" here tracks Jewish penetration of an Arab-majority workforce, the inverse of the Jewish-sector framing above.

Discipline. National cross-employment is census-benchmarked (1921 / 1931 / 1935), not annual — the connecting lines above are visual aids, not interpolated readings. The within-agriculture 35% is a single 1936 datum and must not be read as the economy-wide share. Haifa annual data is genuine but is one city and skews to a government-owned (railway/port) and citrus-export labor scene. Wage tiers are statutory minima, not observed averages. The four-streams account records documented organizational alternatives only; it does not claim any of them commanded majority support — the Hebrew Labor mainstream did.
